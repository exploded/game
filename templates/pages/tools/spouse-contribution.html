The most offset, by your spouse’s income

How it works

You pay after-tax money into your spouse’s super fund as a spouse contribution. When you lodge your tax return, you claim a tax offset of 18% of up to $3,000 of it, so up to $540. If your spouse earns over $37,000, the $3,000 falls by a dollar for each dollar over, to nothing at $40,000. The offset reduces the tax you pay. It isn’t refunded if it’s more than your tax.

About this calculator

What it’s for

It estimates the tax offset you could claim for 2026–27 for putting money into your spouse’s super, how much of it counts, and what it costs you after the offset. It also shows whether your spouse might get the government co-contribution on money they put in themselves.

What it leaves out, and what that means

  • Your own tax. The offset can only reduce tax you pay. If your tax for the year is less than the offset, you get less than shown.
  • Their income is one figure. The calculator takes your spouse’s income as you type it. If you leave something out, the estimate could be too high.
  • Contribution splitting. Moving some of your before-tax contributions to your spouse’s super is a different arrangement and doesn’t count towards this offset.
  • Only this year. It doesn’t show what the money could grow to. The ATO decides what you can claim.
  • The conditions are yours to check. The calculator relies on the boxes you tick. The ATO’s page on spouse contributions has the full rules.
