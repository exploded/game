How it works

Your savings rate does two jobs at once. A higher rate means you invest more each year, and it also means you live on less, so you need a smaller pile to stop.

The model starts from nothing. Each year you invest your savings at the end of the year, and they grow at the real return. You can stop when your savings reach a year's spending divided by the withdrawal rate. Because both sides depend on your pay, the dollar amount drops out and only the rate matters.

The model assumes the same return every year. Real markets rise and fall, and the order of good and bad years matters. It leaves out tax, super and the Age Pension. For an Australian version with super, switch to Add your super.

The method comes from Mr Money Mustache's The Shockingly Simple Math Behind Early Retirement (2012).

Super is locked until preservation age: 60 for anyone born after 30 June 1964. So stopping early has two parts. In the bridge years, from when you stop until 60, you live on money invested outside super. From 60, your super and anything left outside super have to support your spending at the withdrawal rate.

What this leaves out

  • The Age Pension. Leaving it out makes the answer cautious: from Age Pension age, many people get some pension on top of their savings.
  • Income tax on investment earnings outside super.
  • Voluntary super contributions, the contribution caps and the transfer balance cap.
  • Ups and downs in the market. Returns are the same every year.

About this calculator

What it’s for

It shows how your savings rate affects how many years you’d need to work before your savings could cover your spending. “Add your super” adds an Australian estimate of the earliest age that could work, allowing for super being locked until 60.

What it leaves out, and what that means

  • The Age Pension. Leaving it out makes the answer cautious, as many people get some pension from Age Pension age.
  • Tax outside super. Tax on investment earnings outside super would make savings grow more slowly.
  • Voluntary contributions and caps. Extra super contributions, the contribution caps and the transfer balance cap aren’t modelled.
  • A steady return. Returns are the same every year. Real returns rise and fall, and a fall soon after you stop working can make money run out sooner.
