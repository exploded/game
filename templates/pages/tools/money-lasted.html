    How the test works

    The test starts the plan in every month from January 1871 that has a full run of history after it, and steps through it month by month:

    • Take out a month's spending at the start of the month. Spending rises with inflation, so it buys the same each year.
    • Apply that month's real return: the return after inflation is taken out. For shares, that's the US S&P Composite index with dividends reinvested. For bonds, it's worked out from the 10-year US government bond yield.
    • Take off a month's fees.

    The money lasted if it paid every month's spending for the whole period. Balances on the chart are in today's dollars, so a line that ends at the starting balance has kept its buying power.

    Data: Robert Shiller's US stock market data, January 1871 to October 2026. The idea comes from FIRECalc.

    Keep in mind

    • This is the US market. Australian shares and bonds have their own history, and an Australian investor also faces currency moves.
    • The 4% rule comes from US history. Australian research suggests a lower withdrawal rate has been needed to last as often here.
    • Past returns don't predict future ones. The next 30 years could be better or worse than any period in the record.
    • Tax and the Age Pension are left out. For a plan that includes the Age Pension, try Moneysmart's retirement planner.
